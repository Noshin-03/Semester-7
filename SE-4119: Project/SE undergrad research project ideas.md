# Fifteen SE tool projects worth an undergrad's six months

**Recommendation.** The strongest bet for a solo fourth-year student is **SBOMFuzz** (rank 1, score 7.20/10). It is a metamorphic and fault-injection testing framework for SBOM generators. It answers a gap that a 2025 SLR and a September 2026 empirical study state outright, it needs no LLM spending, its oracles are deterministic, and its scoop risk is only medium ([O'Donoghue et al. SLR](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03507v1); [Prado et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19920)). The second pick is **DescCheck** (rank 2, 7.15). It checks whether MCP tools and agent skills disclose the capabilities their code actually has. It is the only candidate whose research problem, not just its tool, the researchers rated "likely clearly novel", and its data overlaps the **MSR 2027 Mining Challenge's GitSkills dataset** (paper due 23 Dec 2026), which gives an early short-paper hedge ([Mattsson et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.10534); [MSR 2027 Mining Challenge](https://2027.msrconf.org/track/msr-2027-mining-challenge)). The third pick is the **interaction-aware PR outcome labeler** (rank 3, 7.05). It is a reusable library that separates real agent failures from workflow closures in agent-authored PRs, and it needs no user study or builds ([Peralta et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.22534)). Security and supply-chain ideas take five of the top fifteen places because they pair stated, verifiable gaps with cheap, automatic ground truth. LLM-testing and agent-PR ideas are just as timely but carry the highest scoop risk. Every score below is an **informed judgment by the report writer, based on the research notes. None is an objective measurement.** Several novelty checks were shallow, so each top-15 idea comes with a list of scoop checks to run before committing.

## How the candidates were found, verified and scored

### Methodology and limitations

Five research notes, one per area, produced 41 viable candidates and 8 "Already done" candidates. The areas were: AI agents and code review, DevOps/CI/MSR, LLM testing and repair, RE/accessibility/green/docs/education, and security/supply chain. A sixth note covered venues and deadlines. Every candidate is rooted in a named source's future-work, limitations, threats or implications section, or in documented practitioner pain. This synthesis uses only papers that have a link or DOI in the notes. Claims carry one of three labels: **[VERIFIED – source]** when the notes read the source's text; **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED]** when only an abstract, a truncated page or a secondary source was read; and **[INFERRED – reasoning]** for the researchers' or this writer's reasoning.

The evidence base has real limits, and the student should know them. The **arXiv export API, the Semantic Scholar API and the DBLP API were blocked** by the session's egress proxy (HTTP 403). **Google Scholar and ResearchGate were not used. ACM DL and IEEE Xplore were not searched directly.** All discovery used a general web search plus page fetches of arXiv HTML/PDF pages, researchr pages and author pages. **Novelty checks per idea often used fewer than the three phrasings asked for:** about 1–3 searches per idea in the RE/a11y/green note, 2–4 in the testing note, about 12 phrasings across the whole DevOps area, and "3+ across the session" in the agents note. Many "closest work" entries were **seen only by title** in search results and their contents are unverified. These are marked "(title/link seen only)". Several long papers were truncated during fetch. Examples: Section 9 of the APR SLR, the implications section of Agent READMEs, the defences section of AIShellJack, and the threats section of the malicious-package paper. Several sources contain internal numeric inconsistencies, which the notes flag: AIDev acceptance rates, the paper counts in the code-review survey, FlakeDetector build counts, and Byam's 27 vs 28 of 103 repairs. Many 2026 preprints state no venue. Dataset URLs for AIDev on HuggingFace, SWE-bench, Defects4J, BugsInPy and IDoFT were **not opened**.

### Scoring rubric

Each candidate gets a score from 1 to 10 on five criteria. **Novelty (25%)**: distance from the closest verified work. **Publication chance (25%)**: the likelihood of acceptance at a realistic venue in 2027. **Feasibility in 6 months (20%)**: solo, part-time, AI-assisted. **Risk (15%, inverse, so 10 means low risk)**: scoop, data and dependency risk. **Relevance (15%)**: practitioner and community interest. The cumulative score is **0.25·N + 0.25·P + 0.20·F + 0.15·R + 0.15·Rel**, out of 10. Two penalties apply. First, when the researchers flagged **HIGH scoop risk**, Risk is capped at 4 or below. Second, when the notes say the **novelty check was incomplete** (for example, the planned phrasings were not run), Novelty is lowered by one point. This applied to OracleCheck (T6), PromptGuard (R1) and DescCheck (S5). Ties are broken by Publication, then Risk, then Feasibility. **These scores are informed judgments, not objective measurements.** Another assessor could reasonably move any single score by ±1. Two ideas whose cumulative scores differ by less than about 0.3 should be treated as equivalent.

### 1. SBOMFuzz: metamorphic and fault-injection testing of SBOM generators (7.20)

**Problem and gap.** SBOM generators disagree badly. Across six tools and 3,287 repositories, tools agreed on detected packages only **7.84–12.77%** of the time, a tool agreed with its own earlier version only about **47–53%** of the time, and supplier and license accuracy was below 20% for every tool [VERIFIED – [Wang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05622)]. On JavaScript, cdxgen covered 98.72% of components against Syft's 43.78% and Trivy's 32.71%, and Syft and Trivy produce **empty SBOMs when there is no lockfile**. The authors' future work asks for Python/Java coverage and for turning the divergence cases into a benchmark [VERIFIED – [Prado et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19920)]. The SBOM SLR explicitly calls for **fault injection to test how generators handle edge cases**, and for completeness metrics modelled on test coverage [VERIFIED – [O'Donoghue et al. §V.C](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03507v1)].

**Proposed tool.** A Python library and CLI with three modules. (1) A project synthesiser emits Python (pip/Poetry/uv) and Java (Maven/Gradle) projects with known dependency truth: lockfile vs no lockfile, extras, optional and dev dependencies, vendored code, git and URL dependencies, workspaces, shaded JARs. (2) A runner executes pinned versions of Syft, Trivy and cdxgen in Docker. (3) An oracle layer compares CycloneDX/SPDX output against ground truth, using differential checks (tool vs tool, version vs version) and metamorphic relations. For example, adding an unused dev dependency must not change runtime components; renaming a directory must not change PURLs; adding a lockfile must not lose components. A GitHub Action pins tool versions and flags regressions. Stack: Python, Docker, the CycloneDX Python library, pytest. No LLM is required; an optional LLM can generate edge-case project variants.

**Research questions.** RQ1: How accurately do current SBOM generators report components, versions/PURLs and fields on projects with constructed ground truth? RQ2: Which metamorphic relations expose the most distinct divergence classes, and how many of them are new compared with Prado's catalogue? RQ3: How stable are tools across consecutive releases (regressions per version)?

**Evaluation plan.** Data: synthesised projects; top PyPI and Maven projects as realistic seeds; Wang et al.'s 660-package Python ground truth if released (unverified). Baselines: plain differential comparison as in Prado/Wang, compared with metamorphic and fault-injected testing. Metrics: per-tool component recall and precision, version/PURL correctness, field correctness (not just presence), number of distinct divergence classes, and regressions found across about three releases per tool. Report issues upstream and count confirmed bugs, a strong practitioner-validation signal [INFERRED].

**Closest work and difference.** Wang et al. measure adherence on real repositories without synthetic ground truth. Prado et al. check field presence only, on JavaScript and Rust. The older Java SBOM-challenges paper ([2303.11102](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.11102)) is descriptive. SBOMFuzz contributes **constructed truth plus metamorphic oracles**, so the benchmark is reusable and can check correctness.

**Venues.** FSE 2027 Tool Demos (26 Jan 2027) is possible only with a prototype; ICST 2027 Testing Tools showcase (12 Feb 2027) fits a testing framework well; EASE 2027 Short Papers (1 Mar 2027); ASE 2027 Tools & Datasets (not yet announced; the 2026 edition accepted SBOM and AI-BOM tools, per [ASE 2026](https://conf.researchr.org/track/ase-2026/ase-2026-tools-and-data-sets)); SCP Software Track or JOSS as a follow-up.

**Risks and mitigations.** The Prado group may release a benchmark: differentiate on Python/Java coverage and metamorphic correctness checks, and release early on Zenodo. Tool churn: pin versions and treat churn as data for RQ3. Scope creep across ecosystems: Python first, Java in M3.

### 2. DescCheck: description-vs-implementation consistency for MCP tools and agent skills (7.15)

**Problem and gap.** Agent extension ecosystems are growing fast and are poorly vetted. Of 31,132 agent skills, **26.1% were flagged**, and skills that bundle scripts had **2.12× the odds** of being flagged [VERIFIED – [Liu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.10338)]. Across 27,111 Skills.sh skills, only **33 were flagged by all five scanners**, and attackers can make documentation look benign while hiding behaviour elsewhere [VERIFIED – [Holzbauer et al. 2026](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.16572.md)]. The ML detector for MCP attacks **analyses only tool descriptions, not tool code** [VERIFIED – [Mattsson et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.10534)]. It assigns AI vs human authorship per test via AIDev metadata and git blame, runs StrykerJS/mutmut (optionally adding LLMorpheus-style mutants), and reports mutation score by author type, with surviving mutants as candidates for test strengthening. The notes suggest **merging it with CovGuard's diff-coverage check** for a stronger tool contribution. Stack: Python, Node, StrykerJS, mutmut, Docker.

**Research questions.** RQ1: Do agent-written tests kill more mutants than human-written tests in the same projects? RQ2: Does assertion density predict kills? RQ3: Which mutant classes survive AI tests?

**Evaluation plan.** Data: AIDev TS and Python subsets ([repo](https://github.com/SAILResearch/AI_Teammates_in_SE3)); Yoshimoto's 10 vitest projects. Metrics: mutation score, kills per test, assertion density vs kills correlation, surviving-mutant taxonomy. Baselines: human tests in the same repository, and EvoSuite/Pynguin-generated tests where applicable.

**Closest work and difference.** Yoshimoto and Dipongkor measure structure and coverage. LLMorpheus ([2404.09952](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.09952v2)) is a mutation tool, not an authorship study.

**Venues.** ICST 2027 Testing Tools (12 Feb); EASE 2027 short (1 Mar); ICSME 2027 tool demo (TBA); AIware 2027 (not announced).

**Risks and mitigations.** Repository builds: Yoshimoto obtained coverage for only 3 of 10 projects. Pre-screen 50 repos and keep those that build. Scoop from the MSR authors: publish an ICST/EASE short paper early.

### 12. ClaimCheck: phantom-change detection in agent PR descriptions (6.60)

**Problem and gap.** 1.7% of AIDev-pop PRs show high message–code inconsistency, with Copilot highest at 8.7%. **Phantom changes, where the description claims work that was never implemented, are 45.4%** of these. High-inconsistency PRs are accepted **28.3% of the time vs 80.0%** and take 55.8h vs 16.0h to merge. The heuristic reached F1 0.630, and the authors call for automated verification [VERIFIED – [Gong et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.04886v2)].

**Proposed tool.** A GitHub App. An LLM splits the description into atomic claims, links each claim to diff hunks and symbols (static diff analysis plus LLM), and posts a checklist of unsupported claims. Stack: Python, unidiff, tree-sitter, an LLM.

**Research questions.** RQ1: Does claim-level verification beat the 0.630 F1 heuristic? RQ2: What does each PR cost? RQ3: Do reviewers find the flags useful (an optional small study)?

**Evaluation plan.** Data: the 974 released annotations (exact URL unverified) plus AIDev-pop. Baselines: the Gong heuristic (0.630), the embedding baseline (0.150), the agreement method (0.567), and the practitioner tools [groundtruth](https://github.com/veltiq/groundtruth) and [hearsay-pr](https://pypi.org/project/hearsay-pr/). Metrics: precision, recall, F1, and cost per PR.

**Venues.** FSE 2027 Tool Demos (26 Jan), very feasible; EASE 2027 short (1 Mar); ICSME 2027 tool demo (TBA).

**Risks and mitigations.** HIGH scoop risk: treat the practitioner tools as baselines to make the evaluation the contribution.

**Milestones.** M1: locate the dataset; run the practitioner tools. M2: claim decomposition; FSE demo. M3: evaluation. M4: App deployment; EASE short. M5: reviewer mini-study. M6: full paper.

**Pre-commitment scoop checks.** Inspect what groundtruth, hearsay-pr and Claim Check actually do and whether they report an evaluation. Locate the PR-MCI data URL. Look for follow-ups by Gong et al.

### 13. CovGuard: a diff-coverage feedback gate for agent PRs (6.60)

**Problem and gap.** Only 49.6% of agent PRs that modify code under test include test changes. Only 22.5% of Python Code+Tests PRs gain diff coverage. Try/catch lines miss coverage 86.0% (Java) and 81.0% (Python) of the time. The authors propose "coverage feedback loops that let agents check whether their own tests exercise their added lines" [VERIFIED – [Dipongkor et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.18057)].

**Proposed tool.** A GitHub Action/CLI. It runs coverage.py on the PR's tests, reports uncovered added or changed lines (with emphasis on except/raise), optionally prompts an LLM or the agent to generate targeted tests, and re-checks. Stack: Python, coverage.py, mutmut, an LLM.

**Research questions.** RQ1: How much does the feedback loop raise diff coverage and error-handling coverage? RQ2: Do the added tests raise mutation score on changed lines? RQ3: What does it cost per PR?

**Evaluation plan.** Data: AIDev Python PRs; Dipongkor's replication package; Python repositories from SWE-bench Verified (link unverified). Baselines: the agent's original tests, plain "write tests for this diff" prompting, and Pynguin. Metrics: diff coverage, error-handling coverage, changed-line mutation score, and cost per PR (DiffTestGen reports about $0.041/PR, [2607.16024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.16024)).

**Closest work and difference.** DiffTestGen targets behavioural differences in human PRs from 4 projects. [covgate](https://docs.rs/crate/covgate/0.1.2) and Codacy skills are unevaluated.

**Venues.** ICST 2027 Testing Tools (12 Feb); EASE 2027 short (1 Mar); ISSTA/ICSME 2027 demos (TBA).

**Risks and mitigations.** HIGH scoop. Builds: Dipongkor could instrument only 34 Python repositories; budget build time accordingly. Merging with MutAudit raises the novelty.

**Milestones.** M1: scoop checks; build pipeline. M2: coverage diff reporter. M3: LLM loop; ICST paper. M4: mutation evaluation; EASE. M5: Action release. M6: full paper.

**Pre-commitment scoop checks.** Read DiffTestGen in full. Inspect covgate, coverctl and Codacy. Look for follow-ups by the Dipongkor/Moran group. Search "coverage feedback coding agent tests".

### 14. CI footprint advisor: estimate and cut CI carbon (6.55)

**Problem and gap.** 2.23M GitHub Actions runs produced an estimated **456.9 MTCO2e**. Network transfer is about 34.8% of carbon, scheduled runs about 33.9% of execution time, and checkout about 6.8% of emissions. Future work: **develop and test reduction strategies** [VERIFIED – [Saavedra et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26413v2)].

**Proposed tool.** A GitHub Action/CLI. It estimates per-workflow footprint with the 2510.26413 methodology (workflow-telemetry plus Electricity Maps), and proposes patches: shallow checkout, caching, `paths:` filters, concurrency cancel, and disabling schedules on inactive repos and forks. Stack: Python, ruamel.yaml, the GitHub API.

**Research questions.** RQ1: What savings do the patches achieve, measured before and after? RQ2: Do maintainers accept footprint PRs? RQ3: Which patches break workflows?

**Evaluation plan.** Data: GHARuns ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23458821)); the 2510.26413 dataset (Zenodo link not captured). Baselines: unmodified workflows. Metrics: minutes and estimated CO2 saved, PR acceptance rate, and breakage rate.

**Closest work and difference.** Software Frugality in CI ([2410.15816](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.15816v1), unread) may cover reduction strategies. The caching study ([2604.13129](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.13129)) is descriptive.

**Venues.** EASE 2027 short (1 Mar); ASE/ICSME 2027 tools (TBA); ICSE 2028 Demo (~Oct 2027, inferred; the ICSE 2027 demo call encouraged discussing carbon footprint); GREENS 2028 (inferred).

**Risks and mitigations.** Estimates are model-based: report uncertainty bounds. Low PR acceptance: target active repositories and offer opt-out.

**Milestones.** M1: read 2410.15816; estimator. M2: patcher. M3: retrospective simulation on GHARuns. M4: PR campaign; EASE short. M5: before/after telemetry. M6: paper.

**Pre-commitment scoop checks.** Read 2410.15816 and 2604.13129 in full. Locate the 2510.26413 Zenodo dataset.

### 15. GHA security ground truth plus meta-scanner and auto-fixer (6.45)

**Problem and gap.** Nine GitHub Actions security scanners run on 2,722 workflows. **No single scanner covers all 10 weakness classes except zizmor**, pinning scanners disagree by thousands of findings (scharf 10,202 vs frizbee 8,020), and **no verified ground truth exists** [VERIFIED – [Fares et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14455)].

**Proposed tool.** A labelled benchmark of injected and real weaknesses, plus a CLI that normalises scanner outputs to SARIF, deduplicates them and builds a consensus, and proposes LLM fixes validated by re-scanning and schema checks. Stack: Python, actionlint/zizmor/poutine, SchemaStore ([schema](https://www.schemastore.org/github-workflow.json)).

**Research questions.** RQ1: What is each scanner's precision and recall on labelled ground truth? RQ2: Does consensus beat the best single scanner? RQ3: What share of LLM fixes are valid and remove the weakness?

**Evaluation plan.** Data: injected weaknesses plus real workflows from the Cardoen MSR 2024 dataset ([replication](https://figshare.com/s/224b0b49967f7ea8283a)). Baselines: each scanner alone. Metrics: precision/recall/F1, fix validity, re-scan pass rate.

**Closest work and difference.** Fares et al. compare scanners without labels. The compliance study ([2605.02091](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.02091)) was seen only.

**Venues.** ICST 2027 Testing Tools (12 Feb); EASE 2027 AI Models/Data (15 Mar); ASE 2027 Tools (TBA).

**Risks and mitigations.** Medium-high scoop from the same group: release the benchmark early. Labelling ambiguity: use two raters.

**Milestones.** M1: scoop checks; weakness-injection templates. M2: run the scanners; normaliser. M3: labelling; ICST paper. M4: consensus layer. M5: auto-fix. M6: paper.

**Pre-commitment scoop checks.** Read 2605.02091 and GHA-Repair (ICSME 2026). Check zizmor's built-in auto-fix features. Look for follow-ups by Fares/Baudry.

## Conclusion

The ranking shows that the best undergraduate projects in late 2026 do not sit in the most crowded spot, LLM agents writing code. They sit where **agents meet infrastructure that nobody has tested**: SBOM generators, skill and MCP manifests, and PR-outcome labels. In those areas a constructed or rule-derived ground truth turns a modest tool into a credible evaluation. The pattern repeats across the notes. The ideas that score highest have cheap, automatic oracles (synthetic projects, seeded mismatches, existing manual labels). The ideas that fall have expensive oracles (user studies, energy hardware, reproducible builds) or sit in the 2026 hot zones where the originating group named the exact tool as its own next step. That makes the "future work" section a double-edged source: it guarantees a citable gap, but it also tells the authors' own students what to build next. **Speed of first publication matters as much as the idea.**

Strategically, the student should commit by mid-December 2026 after running the listed scoop checks, which take about two days on Semantic Scholar/DBLP. The student should also open-source the repository in month 1, which keeps JOSS eligibility open for mid-2027, and plan for two papers rather than one: an early short paper (the MSR Mining Challenge for DescCheck, ICST/EASE for the testing and SBOM ideas) and a full tool paper for the 2027 ASE/ICSME tool tracks once they are announced. For a student who wants the lowest risk, SBOMFuzz is the choice. For one who wants the most novelty and an immediate submission target, DescCheck is the choice. For one who prefers pure data work with no security handling, the PR outcome labeler is the choice.
